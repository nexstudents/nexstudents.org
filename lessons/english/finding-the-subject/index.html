Finding the Subject

Most sentences say the subject first, and then some don't. Here is how to find it when it's hiding behind the verb.

Teacher Notes

Not started yet.

What it is

Most sentences put the subject before the verb, but some turn the order around: a sentence that starts with a place, a question, or a sentence that opens with here or there. The subject is still the one word the sentence is about, it has just moved behind the verb.

Why it matters

A student who can only find a subject by looking at the start of a sentence will get every question and every here or there sentence wrong. It is also the skill behind subject and verb agreement in the lessons that follow, because you cannot make a verb agree with a word you have not found.

What he will get wrong

Choosing here or there as the subject. In Here comes the first light, a student marks here because it comes first. Here and there are never the subject, so ask who or what does the verb, and the answer is light.

What to say when he is stuck

  • Turn the sentence back into natural order by moving the verb behind the subject, and read it out loud.
  • Find the verb first, then ask who or what did it.
  • For a question, answer it in your head as a statement: Where is the rope? becomes The rope is somewhere.

Sometimes the subject comes after the verb. Turn the sentence back around, or find the verb and ask who or what did it.

Most sentences say the subject first. In an inverted sentence the subject follows all or part of the predicate, and you will see it in three places: a sentence that starts with a place, a sentence that starts with here or there, and most questions. The subject is never here or there. If you turn the sentence back into natural order the subject shows itself, and if that doesn't work you can find the verb and ask who or what did it.

Run this test. Turn it around. Put the subject first and the verb after it. If the new sentence says the same thing, the word you moved to the front is the subject.

    The Story, Marked

    The same sentences you just heard. Green is the simple subject and orange is the simple predicate.

    At the end of the dock sits an old green canoe.

    The sentence starts with a place, and the canoe is hiding behind the verb.

    Here comes the first light of the morning.

    Here is never the subject. Ask what does the coming and you get light.

    There are two paddles under the seat.

    There only points at a place. Two describes how many, so paddles is the word.

    Out on the lake glides a single loon.

    Turn it around: a single loon glides out on the lake.

    Worked Examples

    Four done for you. The line under each one says how you could have known.

    Is the water cold yet?

    Say it as a statement and it becomes the water is cold yet. The water is what the question is about.

    Where is the rope?

    Where isn't the subject, it only asks about a place. Turn it around into the rope is somewhere, and the rope is what's left.

    Near the shore floats a red canoe.

    Shore is a noun and it sits right in front of the verb, which makes it a trap. Ask what floats and the answer is canoe.

    There is a heron by the reeds.

    There is never the subject. Find the verb, then ask who or what is there, and the answer is a heron.
