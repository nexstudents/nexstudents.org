How it works

Start at the level just finished, not the level you hope for.

A score that comes out too high is not good news. It just means being taught the wrong things for a year.

The score picks the next move

Sit the level your student has just completed. Where they stop holding up is where they actually are, which is the thing a single test at a single level can never tell you.

  • 85% or above — climb a level
  • Under 60% — drop one
  • In between — that is the placement

Scored by skill

Main idea, inference, vocabulary in context, supporting detail, text structure, author's purpose. Each one scored on its own.

About 20 minutes

One passage, twelve questions, one sitting. Results on screen the moment it is submitted.

Teach the gap, not the grade

Missing all three inference questions means something completely different from three scattered misses. Supporting detail and vocabulary come back quickly. Inference and text structure are the slow ones, and the ones that matter later.

What this does not do

No percentile. We are not going to pretend otherwise.

Saying "your child reads at a 6.4 level" requires a tested reference population of thousands of students. We do not have one. That norming is the actual product the paid tests are selling, and inventing the number without it would be dishonest.

What you get instead is where your student holds up, where they stop, and which skills to teach next — measured against a published skill sequence and against their own earlier results. For deciding what to teach on Monday, that is the more useful of the two. If you need a normed score on paper for your state, a proctored standardized test is still the right tool, and this is not trying to replace it.
